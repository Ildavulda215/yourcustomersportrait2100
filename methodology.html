What this map shows

The map visualizes three interrelated demographic indicators for 120+ countries with projections to 2100:

TFR — total fertility rate — the average number of children a woman is expected to have over her lifetime. The replacement-level threshold is approximately 2.1 children per woman. Below this level, population aging and eventual decline become likely without compensating migration.

Population — the number of people in millions or billions, calculated with fertility, natural increase or decline, and migration flows.

Median age — the age that divides a population into two equal halves: one younger and one older. It is one of the clearest indicators of whether a society is demographically young or old.

Methodology

TFR model: logistic decay

Instead of using a simple linear extrapolation, the map applies exponential decay toward an asymptotic floor, which is more realistic for long-range demographic modeling:

TFR(t) = floor + (TFR₀ − floor) × e^(−r × dt)

The floor is set individually by country group: from 2.0 for many Sub-Saharan African countries, which may stabilize above replacement level, to 0.75 for countries that already have extremely low fertility, such as South Korea or Singapore. This avoids unrealistic results, such as negative fertility rates under a naive linear projection.

Population model: fertility + migration

Population growth is calculated annually through two components:

  • Natural growth: 0.3% + (TFR/2.1 − 1) × 1.5%. When TFR falls below 2.1, this component becomes weaker and may turn negative.
  • Migration: for 35 key countries, net migration assumptions are included, from strong inflows in Gulf states to strong outflows in countries under demographic or political pressure. Migration effects gradually decay to 30% by 2100, reflecting a simplified assumption of long-term global convergence.

Median age model: monotonic aging

Median age does not decrease in the model. The pace of aging is determined by three components:

  • Baseline increase linked to longer life expectancy: +0.07 years per year
  • Sub-replacement fertility effect: max(0; 2.1 − TFR) × 0.1
  • Falling fertility effect: max(0; −ΔTFR) × 0.5

Base data sources

Initial values for fertility and population are based on UN World Population Prospects 2022, World Bank Open Data, and Eurostat. Historical fertility trends use data from 2000–2023.

Why demography is future marketing

Most marketing strategies operate on a 1–3 year horizon. But the most expensive business decisions — entering new markets, building production capacity, investing in brand equity — require understanding who will live where in 20, 30, or 50 years.

Demography is one of the few disciplines that can make half-century forecasts with reasonable stability. People born today will enter the labor market in about 20 years. People who are now 40 will become part of the “silver economy” in roughly 25 years. These people already exist, their approximate numbers are known, and no technological shock can fully erase their demographic weight.

Key marketing implications — starting now

1. Africa is the largest consumer market of the future

Nigeria has around 223 million people today. In this model, it approaches roughly 400 million by 2060 and around 560 million by 2100. For comparison, the entire EU has about 450 million people today, and its population is shrinking.

Nigeria’s median age is 18 years. Most of the country’s population has not yet become fully established consumers: they have not bought their first car, opened their first bank account, selected an insurer, or settled on a preferred smartphone brand. Brands that build loyalty now may acquire customers for the next 40–50 years.

Sub-Saharan Africa as a whole may grow from roughly 1.2 billion people in 2023 to around 2.5–3 billion by 2100 — one of the largest expansions of the consumer class in history.

2. Europe and East Asia: the silver economy

Median age is already 46 years in Germany, 49 years in Japan, and 45 years in South Korea. By 2060, these indicators may move toward the 55–58 range.

These are not simply “stagnating” markets. They are markets with radically different consumption priorities. Health, longevity, financial safety, comfortable aging, travel, and leisure are likely to become structurally more important.

3. The demographic dividend: India ahead of China

India’s median age is 28 years, compared with China’s 39 years. India has already overtaken China in population size and still maintains a younger age structure.

This means India remains in a phase of demographic dividend: the share of working-age people is high, while dependency pressure is still comparatively moderate. For brands, this is a window to become familiar to the world’s largest young middle-class audience.

4. Migration changes consumer markets faster than fertility

Net migration can reshape consumer markets faster than birth rates. Countries such as the UAE, Canada, Australia, Germany, and parts of Western Europe sustain or refresh their consumer base through immigration despite low fertility.

For marketing, this means cultural segmentation and localization cannot rely only on old national stereotypes. In high-migration markets, the audience is often multicultural by design.

5. Russia and Eastern Europe: shrinking markets

Russia and much of Eastern Europe face the challenge of shrinking and aging consumer bases. For business, this often means a shift from pure volume growth toward premiumization, retention, higher ARPU, and deeper customer relationships.

6. TFR below 1.0 is historically unprecedented

South Korea, Singapore, and parts of Southern Europe are near or below extremely low fertility levels. No large modern society has maintained sub-1.0 fertility for many decades without major structural consequences.

If these patterns persist, they will reshape education, real estate, retail, healthcare, finance, and public infrastructure. The opportunity is not only in growing markets, but also in learning how to build products for shrinking ones.

Conclusion

Demography is not a prediction about a distant abstraction. It is a description of people who have already been born and who are already shaping future demand at different life stages.

The map helps identify where money, volume, aging, growth, and contraction are likely to appear. The winners will be those who build brands where audiences have not yet chosen their leaders — in Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia — and those who redesign products for rapidly aging societies — in Europe, Japan, and Korea.

Everything else is tactics.
